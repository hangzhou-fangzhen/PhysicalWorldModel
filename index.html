Abstract

Generated video looks convincing but is not physical ground truth. The Physical World Model takes a scene and predicts how its physical state evolves over time, rather than only what it looks like. This page covers one branch of that effort: fluids. Given a scene, the model infers the physical initial condition and rolls the fluid's physical state forward — pressure, velocity, acceleration, collision response and density ratio — and we draw those fields back onto the video so the physics is visible, not just plausible-looking pixels.

A key stance: the physical quantities are model predictions under explicit scale assumptions. They are not calibrated measurements.

Why a Physical World Model

A scene is more than what it looks like. Beyond visual information, the world also contains physics — how things move, push, deform and settle. That part is not optional to the notion of a world: it is inseparable from it.

Looking real is not the same as being physically real. Today's mainstream world models largely predict appearance only and do not output physical quantities; what they predict is simply too narrow in scope. When a downstream model is handed visual data alone, it has to infer the physics from scratch, which makes carrying out a task substantially harder.

Completing the physical dimension is what lets a downstream model understand and perceive the world more faithfully — and, in turn, produce more sensible action chunks.

What our Physical World Model does

Given an image and a text description, the Physical World Model predicts how the world will evolve and returns the fluid's physical fields alongside the future appearance. In other words, it does not stop at the next frame — it states the physical state that frame is in.

The output video frames and the physical field data can be used to train a VLA, so that the policy gains an explicit understanding of how the physical state of a given scene changes over time, rather than having to guess it from pixels.

Architecture at a glance

The model takes an image and text as input and produces an image, a video and physical fields as output. The physical information is carried through the same forward pass that renders the appearance, so what you watch and what is measured describe the same event.

Method: input condition → physical evolution → frames & data

Input: an image and a text description of the action.

Process: the model combines the scene and the action to infer the physical initial condition, advances the fluid evolution, and then uses the physical information to generate the video.

Initial image + action description → infer physical initial condition → advance fluid evolution → generate video (watchable / editable) plus physical fields and data (comparable / analyzable).

Example: given a reference frame of a plastic bottle and a stemmed glass, and the description “slowly pour orange juice into the glass, then stop pouring”, the model outputs the corresponding pouring process together with the physical state at each moment of that same process.

Stated scope: this release focuses on liquid pouring and refilling in single- or dual-container scenes. Solid contact, multi-object interaction and open-environment navigation are not covered — they are directions for later iterations, not capabilities of this release.

Why fluids first

Fluids are among the hardest cases for appearance-only models: a pour can look right while the dynamics are wrong. The timing of a splash, the shape of a jet and the settling of a free surface all have to be correct together, and none of it can be judged from a single frame. Learning the physical state, not just the appearance, is what lets a result be checked instead of merely watched.

Fluids are also a domain whose physics is well understood, which makes them a sound starting point. So this release is narrowly scoped on purpose — fluid scenes only. The rest of the Physical World Model (rigid bodies, contact, richer interaction) is not shown here.

From physics to data: still analyzable

The output does not stop at “looking”: it can also feed downstream analysis.

  • Per-frame statistics (CSV): records the particle count over time along with the mean and maximum of each physical quantity; for pressure, the standard deviation and minimum are also kept. Well suited to comparing how the numbers change between moments: a value that reaches a numerical bound is reported at that bound, so treat a saturated maximum as an upper bound, not a measured peak, and compare the per-frame mean across moments instead. Note that this is not equivalent to a full spatial distribution.
  • Particle fields (NPZ): retains each particle's position, velocity and physical quantities, usable for further work on spatial distributions, for organising task data, and for research.

Scope & honest caveats

  • Single-phase, incompressible, free-surface fluids only. Steam, two-phase and compressible flow are outside the current scope.
  • The physics is produced by a learned model, not an authoritative reference; it is internally consistent but its agreement with a classical solver has not been certified.
  • Coupling is one-way for now — the fluid is displayed in the scene; it does not yet push the container or a robot.
  • Physical quantities are model predictions under explicit scale assumptions, not calibrated measurements.
  • Reported velocity and pressure extremes are clamped to numerical bounds: where a field saturates, the maximum is the bound rather than a physical peak — use the per-frame mean when a single characteristic number is needed.

What's next

This fluid branch is one step of a larger effort. Next we will cover solid mechanics, starting with quasi-static creep scenarios, and grow the model toward a much wider range of scenes — well beyond fluids.
